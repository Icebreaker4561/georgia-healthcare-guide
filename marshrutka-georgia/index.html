Marshrutka in Georgia: what official advice says, Didube, trains, taxis and motion sickness

A marshrutka is a shared minibus, and many of them leave from Didube in Tbilisi. The Government of Canada, in its advice to its own travellers, says to avoid them, and this guide says so plainly. It also covers where the minibuses start, trains to Batumi and Borjomi, the Tbilisi metro, booked cars, motion sickness on mountain roads and what to do if something goes wrong. Facts come from the Canadian and UK governments, the national parks, Georgian Railway, the NHS and Wikipedia. Sources are at the end.

What a marshrutka is

It is a minibus that strangers share, somewhere between a cab and a route service. In Georgia, official pages mention them on trips out of Tbilisi.

The name. Wikipedia's article on the share taxi lists the "marshrutka in former Soviet countries" among its names. It calls this kind of vehicle "a mode of transport which falls between a taxicab and a bus".

Where you will see one. Two official park pages mention them. Minibuses to Prometheus Cave leave from near Didube metro station. Minibuses to Borjomi run from Didube every day. The details are in the Didube section below.

Our tip. If a route matters to your plans, ask your host or the place you are going how to get there on the day.

What official advice says

The Government of Canada tells its travellers to avoid marshrutkas. Its advice for Georgia was last updated on 27 August 2026.

In its own words. In the section on public transport, the Government of Canada's travel advice says:

Mini-buses called "marshrutka" are poorly maintained and often lack security features like seatbelts. Avoid using them.

Bags and loads. The same section adds two rules. "Avoid storing bags in the overhead compartment or under your seat." And: "Do not take buses that look overloaded or in poor condition."

City transport. The Government of Canada is more positive about the capital: "Buses and the Tbilisi metro are generally safe and reliable, but pickpockets may target tourists."

The UK view. The GOV.UK safety page we checked does not mention minibuses. Its transport advice covers cabs, driving and rail; its cab rules are below.

After dark. For drivers, the Government of Canada advises: "avoid travelling after dark". Our driving in Georgia guide has more on the roads.

What this means. We do not give our own verdict on how risky these minibuses are. We report what two governments say and point to options that official sources describe.

Our tip. If a train covers your route, consider it first. If it does not, plan the road part in daylight.

Didube: where many minibuses leave from

Didube is a metro station in northern Tbilisi. Official park pages name it as the place to catch minibuses to two popular sights.

Prometheus Cave. The national parks' page says the cave "can be accessed from Tbilisi, from the territory near the metro station Didube by mini-buses".

Borjomi. For Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park, the parks write: "Micro-buses are running from Tbilisi Didube subway to Borjomi every day, from 08:00 am to 18:00 pm." Our Borjomi guide covers the town itself.

A transport hub. Wikipedia says the station "serves as a transport hub, serving the district of Didube and other northern neighbourhoods". It also calls it one of the busiest stations in the network.

Didube bus station. According to Wikipedia, "Next to the station is Didube Bus Station, one of the busiest bus stations of Tbilisi, which is used for traveling to mainly western and northern directions of the country." The official park pages speak only of the metro station and the area near it, and we found no official address, opening hours or phone number, so we give none.

Heading into the mountains? Read our Kazbegi guide before you plan the trip north.

If you do ride. Two rules come from official travel advice, the seatbelt check follows GOV.UK's rule for cabs, and the rest are our tips:

  • Pick a seat with a seatbelt, and check that it works. Marshrutkas often lack them.
  • Keep your bag with you, not overhead or under the seat.
  • Do not board a vehicle that looks overloaded or in poor condition.
  • Our tip: plan road trips by day. Official advice for drivers is the same.
  • Our tip: save 112 in your phone before you set off.

Alternatives: trains, city transport and taxis

For several long routes, a train is the main alternative. In Tbilisi, use the metro and city transport; for short trips, book a car.

Trains

On board. Train condition and cleanliness vary greatly from route to route.

Tbilisi to Batumi. This is where the train helps most, with two fast trains every day. People also search for a Tbilisi to Batumi bus. We found no official timetable for one, so we cannot point you to it.

Our tip. Take water and snacks for the whole journey, especially in summer.

Tbilisi metro and buses

Inside the capital, the Tbilisi metro and city routes are the main public transport. Buses and the metro are generally safe and reliable, though pickpockets may target tourists.

Who runs it. The Tbilisi Transport Company runs city transport. Its site, ttc.com.ge, has a Journey Planner, timetables and a Real Time Bus page. It lists four kinds of transport: bus, cable car, subway train and minibus.

Fares. The company's site has a fares page. We do not repeat figures we could not check.

Arriving by air? See our Tbilisi airport guide.

Our tip. At busy stations such as Didube, keep your phone and wallet in a zipped inner pocket.

Cabs

Use an officially marked car, or book one through an app. Both governments give clear rules.

Official taxis. The Government of Canada's travel advice says "taxis are regulated and are recognizable by their white colour and roof-mounted taxi signs". It tells visitors to "use only officially marked taxis", to "never share a taxi with strangers" and to "negotiate the fare in advance if there is no meter".

GOV.UK. The UK's safety advice lists three rules:

  • "do not flag taxis in the street";
  • "try to pre-book taxis through an app";
  • "check the taxi has working seatbelts".

Our tip. Fasten your seatbelt on every ride, in the back seat too.

Motion sickness on mountain roads

Winding roads can make you dizzy or sick. The NHS lists simple steps that help, and a pharmacist can suggest remedies.

What it is. The NHS describes motion sickness as feeling dizzy, or feeling or being sick, "when travelling by car, boat, plane or train". Car sickness is the name many people use for it on the road.

Symptoms. The NHS lists "dizziness, feeling sick (nausea), being sick, headache, feeling cold and going pale, sweating".

What helps. The NHS says:

  • "reduce motion – sit in the front of a car";
  • "look straight ahead at a fixed point, such as the horizon";
  • "breathe fresh air if possible";
  • "break up long journeys";
  • "try ginger", as a tablet, biscuit or tea.

What to avoid. The NHS says "do not read, watch films or use electronic devices". It adds "do not look at moving objects, such as passing cars or rolling waves", and "do not eat heavy meals, spicy foods or drink alcohol shortly before or during travel".

At the pharmacy. According to the NHS, pharmacies sell tablets, patches and acupressure bands. It warns that the bands "do not work for everyone". Patches "can be used by adults and children over 10". A pharmacist can recommend the best option for you or your child.

Our tip. On a minibus, ask for a seat near the front, by a window that opens. Eat a light meal before you go, and keep your phone in your pocket.

If something goes wrong

Dial 112 in an emergency. GOV.UK's health advice says: "Dial 112 and ask for an ambulance."

How to call. Our 112 guide explains how to call and what to say.

Your cover. Visitors need health and accident cover of at least 30,000 GEL for their whole stay; see the entry rule on travel insurance.

A doctor in the capital. For a problem that can wait, see the catalogue of doctors and clinics in Tbilisi on 100doc.ge.

Our tip.

  • Save 112 and your host's number in your phone before a long trip.
  • Carry a list of your medicines and conditions in English.
  • Keep your passport and insurance details on you, not in a bag stored away from your seat.

Frequently asked questions

What is a marshrutka?

A shared minibus. Wikipedia gives "marshrutka" as the name used "in former Soviet countries".

Are marshrutkas a good idea in Georgia?

The Government of Canada's travel advice says they are poorly maintained and often lack seatbelts. It tells visitors: "Avoid using them." We add no verdict of our own.

Where do minibuses leave from in Tbilisi?

The national parks name Didube metro station for minibuses to Prometheus Cave and Borjomi. Wikipedia calls the station a transport hub.

How do I get from Tbilisi to Batumi without a marshrutka?

By train. Georgian Railway lists two fast trains every day. Check times and fares on railway.ge.

How do I take a cab safely in Georgia?

GOV.UK advises pre-booking through an app and not flagging cars in the street. Use only officially marked ones, which are white with a roof sign.

How can I avoid motion sickness on mountain roads?

The NHS says sit in the front, look at the horizon, get fresh air and break up long journeys. Try ginger, and skip screens, heavy meals and alcohol.

What number do I call in an emergency?

Dial 112. GOV.UK says: "Dial 112 and ask for an ambulance."
