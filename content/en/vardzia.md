## Vardzia cave monastery: Queen Tamar's city in the rock {#history}

It is a 12th-century monastery carved into a cliff and consecrated in the reign of Queen Tamar (1184–1213). The [Georgian Encyclopedia](https://georgianencyclopedia.ge/en/form_eng/779) says the multi-tiered (13-level) complex "was constructed mainly between 1156 and 1203, and was consecrated in 1185".

**Where it is.** According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vardzia), the monastery was "excavated from the slopes of the Erusheti Mountain on the left bank of the Kura River, thirty kilometres from Aspindza". The Georgian Encyclopedia lists the museum-reserve's territory in the municipalities of Aspindza, Akhalkalaki and Ninotsminda.

**13 levels or nineteen tiers?** The two sources count differently. The Georgian Encyclopedia describes a 13-level complex. Wikipedia says the rock-cut rooms "stretch along the cliff for some five hundred meters and in up to nineteen tiers". We show both figures rather than pick one.

**What was cut into the rock.** As of 1 January 2010, the Encyclopedia counted 242 caves. Among them are Tamar's cell, a refectory, a wine cellar, a pharmacy, an aqueduct, and hiding places and secret tunnels for defence.

**The earthquake of 1283.** Wikipedia describes a period of partial rebuilding "after heavy damage in the earthquake of 1283". The ground here still moves, as the [rockfalls section](#rockfalls) explains.

**A museum-reserve with monks.** The site has been a museum-reserve since 1938, according to the Encyclopedia. Since 2008 it has been under the National Agency for Cultural Heritage Preservation of Georgia. Wikipedia adds that "Several monks still live onsite".

**UNESCO.** Wikipedia says the wider Vardzia–Khertvisi area "has been submitted for future inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List". It has not been inscribed.

## Visiting the caves: what to see {#visit}

The main church is the Church of the Dormition, known for its wall paintings. The Encyclopedia says the murals of the church and its narthex (1184–1203) show scenes from the life of Christ and include "portraits of King Giorgi III and Queen Tamar".

**Tunnels and passages.** Wikipedia notes that the "infrastructure includes access tunnels, water facilities, and provision for defence". The section near the Secret Tunnel was closed in 2026, so ask at the entrance which parts are open on the day.

**The bell tower.** A 13th-century bell tower is part of the ensemble, according to the Encyclopedia.

**Fifteen churches.** The Encyclopedia counts 15 churches in total. Besides the main church, they include the Ananauri Church, with murals from the 13th to 15th centuries.

**Monks and worship.** This is still a working monastery. Our tip: cover shoulders and knees, keep your voice down in the churches and ask before taking photos of people.

**Hours and tickets.** We do not quote opening hours or prices: we found no official English source for them. Check on arrival.

**Our tip: what to wear and carry.**

- Shoes with a good grip: the route has stairs, ledges and uneven rock.
- Water and a hat: much of the walk is on an open cliff face.
- A small backpack rather than a bag, so you can hold the rail with both hands.

## Rockfalls and the 2026 closure near the Secret Tunnel {#rockfalls}

Part of the site was closed to visitors in 2026 after rocks fell. On 15 September 2026, [Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/vardzia-section-temporarily-closed-after-rockfalls-restoration-works-to-begin/) reported a statement from the National Agency for Cultural Heritage Preservation of Georgia: "Periodic rockfalls are occurring at Georgia's Vardzia Museum-Reserve due to its location in a seismically active zone."

**What happened.** In the report's words: "Earlier this year, increased rockfall activity near the so-called 'Secret Tunnel' damaged a pedestrian footbridge and protective handrails, prompting the temporary closure of the section to visitors."

**What is being done.** The agency said "protective netting has been installed in high-risk areas", while specialists continue to monitor the site. Its experts and geologists from the Georgian Technical University surveyed the area and assessed the risk of further rockfalls there "as minimal". Debris removal, more netting and repairs to the handrails were to begin "in the coming days".

**When it reopens.** The report says: "Full visitor access to Vardzia will be restored once the works are completed." It gives no date. Ask at the entrance whether the section is open again.

**Earthquakes.** The UK's [safety advice for Georgia](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/georgia/safety-and-security) is short on this: "There are frequent small earthquakes in Georgia." The heritage agency links the rockfalls at the monastery to its seismically active setting.

**Our tip: on the cliff.**

- Stay on open paths with railings. Do not climb over barriers, fences or netting.
- Follow what the staff tell you, even if a closed path looks safe.
- Do not stop to rest under overhanging rock or next to fresh stones on the path.
- If the ground shakes, move away from the edge and from loose rock, then follow staff instructions.

## Rabati Castle in Akhaltsikhe {#rabati}

Rabati Castle is the fortress of Akhaltsikhe, the administrative centre of the Samtskhe–Javakheti region, according to Wikipedia. Wikipedia, which also calls it Akhaltsikhe Castle, records a modern restoration of the complex.

**Our tip.** If you spend a night in Akhaltsikhe, walk around the castle in the evening and keep the next day for the monastery. We do not quote ticket prices; check at the gate.

## Getting to Vardzia: Akhaltsikhe, Khertvisi and Borjomi {#getting-there}

Plan the trip around Akhaltsikhe, with Rabati Castle and clinics. Wikipedia puts the monastery thirty kilometres from Aspindza.

**Times and fares.** We do not give bus times, fares or driving times. The figures we found online were not confirmed by an official source. Ask your host or the bus station the day before.

**Khertvisi.** Khertvisi fortress belongs to the Vardzia–Khertvisi area that, according to Wikipedia, was submitted to UNESCO together with the monastery.

**From Borjomi.** If you are based in the spa town, see our [guide to Borjomi](https://icebreaker4561.github.io/georgia-healthcare-guide/borjomi/).

**Our tip.** Save an offline map and the names Aspindza and Akhaltsikhe before you leave. The map works if the signal drops, and you will need to say where you are if you call for help.

## Medical help near Vardzia {#health}

The monastery is in a rural area, and serious care is in the cities. The UK's [health advice for Georgia](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/georgia/health) says: "Outside Tbilisi, medical facilities are limited."

**Rural areas.** Canada's [travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/georgia), updated 27 August 2026, says: "In rural areas, there are shortages of medical supplies and personnel." It adds: "First responders may not be able to access rural areas." Plan for help to take time.

**In an emergency.** Dial 112. The UK advice says: "Dial 112 and ask for an ambulance. Contact your insurance company quickly if you're referred to a medical facility for treatment." Here is [how to call an ambulance on 112](https://icebreaker4561.github.io/georgia-healthcare-guide/ambulance-georgia/) and what to say. Our tip: tell the operator the name of the monastery, the municipality (Aspindza) and the level you are on.

**Clinics in Akhaltsikhe.** Akhaltsikhe is the regional centre. The catalogue of [clinics in Akhaltsikhe](https://100doc.ge/en/akhaltsikhe/) on 100doc.ge listed 5 clinics when we checked on 19 November 2026.

**Your cover.** Visitors need health and accident cover of at least 30,000 GEL for their whole stay; see [the entry rule on travel insurance](https://icebreaker4561.github.io/georgia-healthcare-guide/#entry-insurance).

**Other risks.** For roads and crime, read [Is Georgia safe?](https://icebreaker4561.github.io/georgia-healthcare-guide/is-georgia-safe/)

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### What is Vardzia?

A 12th-century monastery carved into a cliff in southern Georgia, begun under King Giorgi III and consecrated under Queen Tamar. Today it is a museum-reserve, and Wikipedia says several monks still live there.

### Who built it, and when?

The Georgian Encyclopedia dates the main construction to 1156–1203 and the consecration to 1185, under Queen Tamar. The murals of the Church of the Dormition show her and King Giorgi III.

### How many levels and caves are there?

The Encyclopedia describes 13 levels and counted 242 caves as of 1 January 2010. Wikipedia gives up to nineteen tiers along some five hundred metres of cliff.

### Is the whole site open?

Not necessarily. A section near the so-called Secret Tunnel was closed earlier in 2026 after rockfalls. On 15 September 2026 Georgia Today reported that full access will be restored once repairs are done; no date was given. Ask at the entrance whether it has reopened.

### Is it safe to visit?

The heritage agency links the rockfalls to a seismically active zone and has put up protective netting in high-risk areas; in September 2026 it said geologists had assessed the risk of further rockfalls in the surveyed area as minimal. Our tip: stay on open, railed paths.

### Is there an entrance fee?

We do not quote prices because we found no official English source. Check on arrival.

### Where can I find a doctor nearby?

The nearest regional town with clinics in our catalogue is Akhaltsikhe. In an emergency, dial 112 and ask for an ambulance.
